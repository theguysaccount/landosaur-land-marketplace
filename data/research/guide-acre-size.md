# How big is an acre, really?

An acre measures area, not shape, frontage or useful building space. Two parcels containing the same acreage can offer completely different layouts. Before deciding whether a property is large enough, translate its area into a rough drawing of the things you want to fit, then check that drawing against the actual boundary and constraints.

## Convert area, then consider shape

An acre contains 43,560 square feet. A square of that area has sides about 208.7 feet long, but an acre need not be square or rectangular. One hectare is approximately 2.471 acres. These conversions come from NIST’s measurement tables. [NIST units of measurement](https://www.nist.gov/system/files/documents/2021/11/30/2022-HB44-Section-Appendix-C.pdf).

For a hypothetical example, a rectangle 100 feet wide and 435.6 feet long contains one acre. So does a square about 208.7 feet on each side. The narrow rectangle may have much less flexibility after a driveway, setbacks and a wastewater area are considered. Neither the conversion nor the drawing says what a local authority will permit.

Area also says little about the distance you must maintain. A long, narrow parcel can require more fence per acre than a compact parcel. Make a separate estimate of boundary length, driveway length and utility trench length. Those are linear quantities; multiplying a per-acre allowance by the advertised acreage will not reliably capture them.

## Understand where the acreage came from

A listing can repeat a tax record, a deed statement, a survey calculation or a measurement traced on a web map. Record the source rather than averaging inconsistent figures. If the difference affects price, financing or the layout, ask a qualified surveyor to explain it before committing.

Older records also require attention to measurement conventions. NIST documents the retirement of the U.S. survey foot for new applications after 2022 while explaining its continuing relevance to historical data. Do not relabel an old coordinate file or change its units without checking the coordinate system and record context. [NIST U.S. survey foot](https://www.nist.gov/pml/us-surveyfoot).

BLM’s cadastral resources include official survey tools and Public Land Survey System data. Those resources help interpret records; a downloaded grid is not a newly commissioned survey of your private parcel. [BLM cadastral tools](https://www.blm.gov/programs/lands-and-realty/cadastral-survey/cadastral-tools).

## Make acreage answer a practical question

Create a sketch showing the proposed building, access, parking, wastewater area, water supply, open space and future expansion. Label all dimensions as approximate until the relevant work verifies them. Add mapped constraints without treating their displayed edges as survey-grade boundaries.

Ask: Does the required layout fit inside the actual usable envelope? Can vehicles turn? Is there room to maintain the systems? Does the purchase include every parcel shown? Is a road or access strip counted in the total?

Use the measurements to check acreage assumptions in your [property analysis](/analyze), and retain the worksheet with the survey records. If the answer depends on a boundary’s exact position, scope a survey through the [professional services workspace](/services). More precise arithmetic cannot repair an uncertain boundary.
