# Read land price history without inventing a trend

A property's history can contain asking prices, auction minimums, financed offers, assessments and recorded transfers. Putting those numbers on one line creates a convincing chart even when they describe different things. The useful question is what changed, when it changed, and whether the evidence supports a market conclusion.

## Keep three histories separate

An offer history records what a source advertised. A transaction history records transfers and any verified consideration. A market series summarizes a defined population. None automatically substitutes for the others.

If a seller changes a listing from $60,000 cash to $5,000 down, the land has not necessarily fallen 92% in price. If a deed is recorded with nominal consideration, that is not necessarily an arm’s-length sale at that amount. If a listing disappears, record its removal or last observation; do not manufacture a closing date or sold price.

For each observation, retain the source URL, capture date, stated effective date, price kind, parcel set and ownership interest. Keep the same-parcel match provisional if identifiers or legal descriptions conflict. A subdivision can make a later price refer to a smaller property even when the street location looks unchanged.

## Understand the statistical series

USDA NASS publishes separate agricultural land-value charts for farm real estate, cropland and pasture. The categories are meaningful and should remain visible when a series is used. A national agricultural chart should not be relabeled as the history of all vacant land or of a particular homesite. [USDA land-value charts](https://data.nass.usda.gov/Charts_and_Maps/Land_Values/index.php).

Read the methodology alongside the chart: population, reference period, collection method and quality measures determine what can be inferred. NASS provides its methodology and quality materials by year. Use the version corresponding to the data you are comparing. [USDA methodology archive](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Methodology_and_Data_Quality/Land_Values/index.php).

## A hypothetical interpretation

Suppose an aggregator observed a 40-acre offering at $120,000 in March and $105,000 in June. In August a county record shows a transfer, but no reliable sale consideration is available. The supported statements are a $15,000 reduction in observed asking price and a later recorded transfer. The evidence does not establish a $105,000 closing, the buyer’s concessions or a realized investment return.

Now suppose a county listing median declines during the same period because several small serviced lots leave the inventory while large remote tracts enter it. The median can move even if no individual property’s price changes. Compare stable cohorts and show their counts before calling that movement a market decline.

## Questions before using a trend

- Are the prices the same kind and the parcels the same size and interest?
- Are values nominal dollars or adjusted for inflation, and is that choice disclosed?
- Does the chart track a stable sample or changing available inventory?
- Are closed sales independently supported, with unusual transfers excluded or identified?
- Is the sample large and recent enough for the proposed conclusion?
