# Land loans and construction loans fund different stages

Buying a parcel and completing a usable project are separate funding problems. A lender willing to finance land may not fund a future building, and a construction lender may require evidence that the site and project are ready. Plan the transition before committing all available cash to the initial purchase.

## Draw the funding sequence

Start with four stages: acquire the land, resolve feasibility, build the improvements and move into long-term ownership. For each stage, record the cash needed, likely timing, proposed funding source and conditions that must be satisfied. Include the period when you may carry land debt while paying for plans, permits and site investigations.

A construction facility commonly funds a sequence of work rather than delivering the whole budget on day one. CFPB’s construction disclosure guidance distinguishes construction-only financing from financing that converts to a permanent phase. It also explains that covered transactions may disclose those phases separately or together. The actual lender and transaction determine the documents and conditions. [CFPB construction-loan FAQs](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/compliance/compliance-resources/mortgage-resources/tila-respa-integrated-disclosures/tila-respa-integrated-disclosure-faqs/).

## Ask what unlocks each advance

Request the lender’s requirements for ownership, access, utilities, permits, plans, builder approval, insurance, inspections and contingency funding. Ask how the lender values land already owned and whether existing land debt must be repaid at construction closing. Do not treat a preliminary conversation as a commitment to lend.

Understand the draw process: who requests funds, what evidence is required, when reimbursement occurs and how disputes or cost overruns affect further advances. Match that process to the builder’s payment schedule. A project can appear fully funded on paper while still requiring cash to bridge payments between work and reimbursement.

## A hypothetical timing problem

Suppose a buyer has $90,000 available and uses $75,000 to acquire land. Preliminary plans and site work then consume $12,000. Only $3,000 remains when a construction proposal requires a separate reserve and buyer contribution. The issue is not necessarily the parcel’s value; it is the timing and conditions of the next loan.

A better worksheet shows the entire sequence before closing. Run a delay case, an overrun case and a case where permanent financing requires requalification. Ask exactly when rates, fees and commitments expire instead of assuming today’s proposal lasts through the build.

## Match the program to the purpose

Agricultural lending is another category. USDA Farm Service Agency ownership loans support eligible farm and ranch acquisitions and improvements, with borrower and repayment requirements. An agricultural program is not a universal financing option for recreational acreage or a rural home project. [FSA farm ownership loans](https://www.fsa.usda.gov/resources/loans/farm-ownership-loans).

Compare written proposals using total cash requirements, debt balances, draw costs, maturity dates and conversion conditions. Keep acquisition value separate from a hypothetical completed value. Use [analysis](/analyze) to compare overall cost assumptions, while maintaining the staged draw schedule separately; the calculator is not a construction cash-flow scheduler. Use [services](/services) to organize the planning, engineering and closing work lenders need. A financing plan is ready when its dependencies and cash gaps are visible, including the possibility that the project proceeds more slowly than hoped.
