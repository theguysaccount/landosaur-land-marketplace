# A road on the map is not the whole access story

An aerial image may show a track reaching a parcel. That is evidence of a visible route, not enough evidence that you can lawfully use it, improve it or rely on it year-round. Investigate legal rights, physical conditions and maintenance obligations as three connected questions.

## Trace the entire route

Start at a recognized public road and follow the proposed route to the actual parcel. Identify every intervening ownership and every document that supports crossing it. Ask the relevant road authority where public responsibility begins and ends. A road name on a consumer map does not answer that question.

Obtain copies of the recorded access instruments and any plats or maintenance agreements they reference. Have a qualified professional review the benefited property, burdened property, route, width, permitted users and uses, duration, gates, relocation provisions and maintenance allocation. Utility installation may require different rights from travel.

Alaska DNR’s land-sales access guidance illustrates why physical and legal access must be separated: access can involve different rights-of-way or other routes, and buyers may need to construct approaches and obtain permits. Those examples concern Alaska state offerings; they are not a guarantee of access elsewhere. [Alaska DNR access guidance](https://dnr.alaska.gov/mlw/landsales/info/access/).

## Match access to the proposed use

A seasonal recreational route may be inadequate for a year-round dwelling, construction deliveries or emergency response. Ask the permitting authority and service providers what standards apply to the actual project. Check grades, turning space, bridge limits, drainage crossings and seasonal closures with the professionals responsible for those questions.

Arrange lawful site access before visiting. Do not assume that a seller’s invitation authorizes crossing someone else’s land. Photograph observed conditions and record the season and weather; a dry summer visit may not explain a spring washout or winter closure.

## A hypothetical access budget

Imagine a $40,000 parcel reached by an existing half-mile track. A preliminary review finds that recorded travel rights exist, but the proposed home requires a different driveway design and a drainage crossing. A contractor’s initial allowance adds $28,000 before other site costs. The property may still work, but its access is no longer a zero-cost feature.

If the rights themselves remain disputed, do not turn the uncertainty into a small arbitrary discount. Identify the document or resolution needed and make the purchase decision conditional on that evidence.

## What a useful access file contains

- A route sketch linked to the exact parcels and recorded instruments.
- Written confirmation of public-road status where relevant.
- A professional explanation of rights and unresolved exceptions.
- A feasible improvement and maintenance scope, with cost responsibility.
- Any required driveway, crossing or utility approvals and their status.

Title insurance can involve access coverage and exclusions; the actual policy matters. Texas’s insurance regulator describes access-related defects and emphasizes reviewing policy terms. It provides a concrete example, not a universal coverage promise. [Texas title-insurance FAQ](https://www.tdi.texas.gov/title/titlefaqs.html).
