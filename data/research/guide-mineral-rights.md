# Surface ownership and mineral rights can be different

A land purchase may convey the surface while someone else owns some or all of the minerals underneath. The phrase “mineral rights included” also needs evidence: which minerals, what fraction, subject to which leases or reservations? Resolve the interest being offered before assigning value to possible extraction income.

## Establish the chain of rights

Ask the seller and title professionals for the documents supporting the claimed mineral interest. Review reservations, prior conveyances and existing leases with a professional qualified in the relevant jurisdiction and resource. Surface ownership shown in a tax database may not identify mineral ownership or the full chain of interests.

BLM describes split estate as surface and subsurface rights owned by different parties. Its oil-and-gas guidance addresses federal mineral interests under privately held surface and explains the associated management framework. It should not be treated as a complete statement of private mineral law in every state. [BLM split-estate guidance](https://www.blm.gov/programs/energy-and-minerals/oil-and-gas/leasing/split-estate).

Original federal patents can matter. BLM’s discussion of Stock Raising Homestead Act lands explains that some patents reserved minerals to the United States and recommends examining the patent and relevant land records. The exact reservation, rather than a modern marketing description, is the starting evidence. [BLM mineral reservations](https://www.blm.gov/programs/energy-and-minerals/mining-and-minerals/split-estate).

## Separate ownership from economic potential

Owning an interest does not establish commercially recoverable resources, an available operator, permission to develop or future income. Request the basis of any revenue claim: existing production statements, lease terms, deductions, remaining term and the precise share conveyed. Treat speculative mineral potential separately from the value of the surface use you can support today.

Likewise, a royalty interest, a mineral ownership interest and a working interest are not interchangeable labels. Ask counsel and an appropriate technical professional to identify the rights and obligations of the actual asset. Do not convert an unexplained percentage into an income estimate.

## Investigate effects on the surface

For existing or possible development, ask about access routes, facilities, operational rights, notices, surface-use agreements, reclamation and liabilities. Check whether the intended house, grazing operation or conservation plan can coexist with those rights. Examine the actual agreements and regulatory records rather than assuming a clean-looking site has no continuing obligations.

**Hypothetical example:** a buyer values a recreational parcel for quiet use, while a recorded mineral lease permits certain operational activity. Even if expected royalty income is small or unknown, the lease can matter to that buyer’s use. The sensible response is to understand the rights and realistic operational context before deciding whether the parcel fits.

## Define a useful professional assignment

Request a written ownership summary identifying source documents, interest fractions, reservations, active agreements and unresolved gaps. If income is material to the purchase, commission a separate technical and economic review with explicit assumptions. Ask what the title policy covers and excludes regarding minerals.

Keep the surface and mineral scenarios separate in [analysis](/analyze), and use [services](/services) to scope specialized work. The purchase should make sense on the rights and evidence actually available, without needing an unverified mineral windfall to justify its price.
