# Read an owner-financing offer before the payment

Owner financing describes who provides the credit. It does not, by itself, describe when you receive title, how the debt is secured or what happens after a missed payment. The practical decision is whether the complete agreement fits your intended use and ability to pay through its final deadline.

## Identify the legal structure

Ask whether the transaction conveys a deed at closing with a mortgage or deed of trust, uses a contract for deed with title transferred later, or follows another structure. Obtain the proposed documents before making a nonrefundable commitment. Local counsel should explain the structure and the applicable state rules for the actual property.

CFPB’s 2024 report examines contracts for deed in home purchases, including delayed title and risks involving liens, taxes and forfeiture. That historical research explains why the document structure matters. It should not be read as a current legal ruling that every vacant-land contract has the same protections or remedies. [CFPB contract-for-deed report](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_contract-for-deed_report_2024-08.pdf).

## Review the debt and the property together

Ask for the full price, initial payment, financed balance, interest calculation, installment schedule, fees, prepayment terms and final balance. Request an amortization schedule that matches the signed contract. Confirm who services the account and how you will receive payment receipts, current balances and payoff instructions.

Then investigate the seller’s authority to sell, the title record, existing debt and other interests. Have advisers explain how existing obligations will be handled and what evidence will confirm their release when required. A manageable monthly payment does not solve an unresolved ownership or access problem.

## Test the final payment

**Hypothetical example:** your budget can support a $600 monthly payment, but the proposed contract also requires $35,000 after five years. A plan that works only if a future lender refinances the land is conditional. Test a second scenario in which refinancing is unavailable and resale takes longer than expected.

CFPB notes that a balloon is a substantial payment due at the end of a loan and that refinancing can become unavailable. Use that principle to evaluate liquidity without assuming any particular lender will approve you later. [CFPB balloon-payment guidance](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-balloon-payment-when-is-one-allowed-en-104/).

## Put responsibilities in writing

- Who pays taxes, insurance, association charges and assessments, and how is payment verified?
- When may you occupy, improve, lease or resell the property?
- What notices, cure periods, acceleration rights and remedies apply after default?
- What happens if the seller dies, transfers the contract or has other creditors?
- Which documents are recorded, and who confirms recording and the final title transfer?

Ask these questions before spending on a driveway, well or building. An improvement budget must account for your rights at that stage of the agreement.

For fixed-rate monthly financing, use the [analysis workbench](/analyze) to test amortization and balloon assumptions. Retain the signed payment schedule separately, especially if its terms differ from that model. Scope a document and title review through [services](/services). The useful outcome is a transaction you can explain from the first payment through the deed and debt release, with unresolved terms visible before you commit.
