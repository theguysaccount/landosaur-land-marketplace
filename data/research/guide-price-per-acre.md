# Price per acre is only the first comparison

A low price per acre can identify an interesting property, but it cannot tell you whether the property is inexpensive for your intended use. The denominator may include roads, steep ground, restricted areas or land that contributes little to the project. Start with the whole purchase, then explain which acres make that purchase useful.

## Define the comparison before calculating it

Record the complete cash asking price and the acreage source. Keep surveyed acreage, assessor acreage and the seller’s advertised acreage as separate observations when they disagree. A deposit, opening bid or financed down payment does not belong in a cash-price-per-acre comparison.

Then select an appropriate comparison group. Similar geography alone is insufficient: a serviced homesite, an agricultural tract and a recreational holding can have different buyers and cost structures. USDA’s land-value publications have defined agricultural categories and survey methods. Their averages provide context; they do not price an individual parcel. [USDA NASS methodology](https://www.nass.usda.gov/Publications/Methodology_and_Data_Quality/Land_Values/index.php).

## Keep more than one denominator

For the same property, show gross acres, acres plausibly usable for the proposed purpose and the evidence supporting that usable-area estimate. Treat an early desktop estimate as provisional. Do not silently remove wetlands or steep areas and then imply the remaining land has received development approval.

For a homesite, a feasible house, wastewater system and driveway layout may matter more than average acreage. For farmland, productive acres and expected net land income matter. Iowa State’s economic framework evaluates expected revenue after operating and land-ownership costs, including management; it is a useful way to avoid treating gross farm revenue as land income. [Iowa State Extension](https://www.extension.iastate.edu/Agdm/wholefarm/html/c2-76.html).

## A hypothetical comparison

Imagine Parcel A costs $80,000 for 20 acres. Parcel B costs $70,000 for 10 acres. Their advertised ratios are $4,000 and $7,000 per acre. Suppose preliminary work suggests A has four acres suitable for your intended layout and needs $45,000 of access and service work; B has six potentially suitable acres and needs $15,000.

The provisional acquisition-plus-work totals become $125,000 and $85,000. Dividing those totals by the provisional useful areas gives $31,250 and about $14,167 per acre. These invented figures demonstrate the denominator problem; neither number is an appraisal or a verified construction budget. The final choice could still favor A for privacy, recreation or a different use.

## Build a comparison you can update

- Retain the original asking price, price kind, acreage and observation date.
- List site costs individually, with a quote or an explicitly labeled allowance.
- Record which usable-area assumptions require a survey, designer or authority’s response.
- Compare the same rights and the same stage of readiness across properties.
- Recalculate when better evidence arrives, rather than preserving an attractive first impression.

Use [land-price context](/land-prices) to understand what a dataset measures, then put your property-specific costs in the [analysis workbench](/analyze). Your decision should survive changing the denominator and adding the work that makes the land useful.
