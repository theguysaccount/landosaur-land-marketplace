# Evaluate a well as a water system

“Well on property” does not tell you whether a proposed home, irrigation plan or livestock operation has a dependable water supply. A well has a legal context, a physical construction, a capacity and a water-quality profile. Evaluate those separately, then combine them into an operating plan.

## Identify the source and rights

Obtain the well log, location, construction and completion records, maintenance history and any permits or water-right documents. Confirm whether the system is private, shared or connected to a wider service. If shared, review the agreement for capacity allocation, maintenance, power, access and repair decisions.

Ask the relevant state or local water authority what rights and approvals apply to the intended use. Permission for household supply does not automatically answer irrigation or commercial-use questions. A nearby well, a seller’s recollection or a map symbol is useful context but not proof of your parcel’s entitlement or available supply.

EPA’s private-well resources describe the responsibilities and resources relevant to private drinking-water owners. Use them to locate the appropriate program and testing support rather than assuming a private system receives the same routine oversight as a public water utility. [EPA private wells](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells).

## Separate quantity from quality

Ask a qualified well professional to evaluate condition and an appropriate supply test for your planned demand. Record test duration, pumping conditions, observations and limitations. A short demonstration that a faucet runs does not establish a dependable seasonal supply.

Use a certified laboratory for drinking-water testing and ask the health authority which contaminants should be included for the location and history. EPA recommends routine testing for specified indicators and additional testing when conditions or nearby activities warrant it. A clear appearance or pleasant taste does not establish water quality. [EPA well-water protection and testing](https://www.epa.gov/privatewells/protect-your-homes-water).

Keep the sample date, collection method and laboratory report. A clean historical sample answers a historical question; it does not guarantee unchanged conditions after flooding, repairs or land disturbance.

## Model the delivered-water cost

**Hypothetical example:** a parcel has an existing well, but the pump needs replacement, the proposed home is distant from the wellhead and treatment is recommended. Your usable-water budget must include equipment, trenching, power, storage if needed, treatment, testing and continuing maintenance. Counting the well as a zero-cost utility would conceal most of the decision.

For undeveloped land, request drilling and system proposals with their assumptions, exclusions and outcomes if the initial attempt is unsuccessful. Treat neighboring logs as supporting context; do not describe a future well’s yield or quality as guaranteed.

## Define the acceptance decision

Write down the intended demand, required evidence, acceptable system cost and fallback options before ordering work. Coordinate the well location with septic design, access and the building layout. Ask who will maintain the system and how quickly repairs can be obtained.

Scope the work through [services](/services), and put initial and recurring costs into [analysis](/analyze). A water supply is ready for a purchase decision when its legal basis, tested condition, practical capacity and treatment obligations are documented for the intended use.
