# Standing trees are not a timber valuation

A wooded parcel can have recreation, habitat, privacy and timber value at the same time. The value of a potential harvest depends on more than tree cover or total acreage. You need evidence of the merchantable resource, the practical ability to harvest it and the market that can purchase it.

## Begin with your ownership objectives

State what you want the forest to look like and do after any harvest. Income, habitat, fire management, recreation and long-term growth may point toward different treatments. A proposal that maximizes immediate receipts may not match the property you intend to own afterward.

Ask an independent forester to scope an inventory or timber cruise appropriate to the decision. Relevant findings may include species, size, quality, volume, health, access and operational constraints. Record the measurement method and uncertainty instead of treating a seller’s estimate as verified inventory.

The USDA Forest Service explains that timber prices vary with species, quality, volume, location and harvesting conditions, and recommends professional assistance in planning a sale. Its guidance is a foundation for the process rather than a current price quote for your stand. [USDA Forest Service timber-sale guidance](https://www.srs.fs.usda.gov/econ/data/prices/wannano.htm).

## Connect inventory to an actual sale

Identify the product mix and plausible local purchasers with the forester. Ask how distance, roads, terrain, season, equipment and the sale’s size affect marketability. Distinguish standing timber value from prices for logs delivered to a mill; transportation and harvesting responsibilities can make those numbers incomparable.

Review boundaries, access rights, existing timber contracts, restrictions and applicable operating requirements. Confirm who owns the timber rights being offered and whether any harvest obligation or reservation survives the land sale. Aerial tree cover cannot answer those ownership questions.

## Calculate what remains after harvest

**Hypothetical example:** a preliminary sale scenario produces $50,000 in gross receipts. Assume $5,000 of sale administration and professional work plus $12,000 of required road restoration and regeneration work. The provisional amount is $33,000 before income taxes and other applicable costs. If the quoted price already reflects particular harvesting expenses, do not deduct the same expenses again.

Next value the residual property in the condition the scenario actually leaves. Do not add a full standing-timber value to comparable sales that already include a similar stand, and then count the harvest proceeds a second time. Land value and timber removal must be modeled consistently.

Tax treatment also depends on ownership, basis and the transaction. The Forest Service publishes timber-tax resources by tax year. Use the relevant year and qualified advice rather than converting a general tip into a guaranteed deduction or tax rate. [Forest Service timber tax resources for 2025](https://research.fs.usda.gov/srs/articles/timber-tax-tips-2025).

## Specify the deliverables

Request an inventory summary, management recommendation, sale structure, operational conditions, restoration obligations and a range of net outcomes. Preserve the reports and actual post-harvest records so future decisions start with the changed forest.

Use [services](/services) to scope the forestry assignment and [analysis](/analyze) to compare holding and harvest scenarios. A credible timber estimate links the stand, rights, market and future property condition; it does not multiply wooded acres by a generic value.
