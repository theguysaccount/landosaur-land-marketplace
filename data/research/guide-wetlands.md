# Wetland screening is the start of a site decision

Wetland information can change a driveway route, a building envelope, a farming plan or a property's conservation value. The important distinction is between a mapped indication, a field finding and the regulatory treatment of a proposed activity. Keep all three visible instead of reducing the issue to a yes-or-no listing label.

## Use inventory maps for screening

The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service’s National Wetlands Inventory uses a biological wetland definition. The agency explicitly says its mapping does not establish the presence, absence or extent of wetlands covered by federal, state, tribal or local law. A blank area on that map is not a clearance to alter land. [FWS Wetlands Mapper](https://www.fws.gov/program/national-wetlands-inventory/wetlands-mapper).

Record the map source, date and limitations, then compare it with aerial imagery, terrain, visible drainage and the proposed work. Use that review to decide where professional field investigation is needed. Avoid drawing an exact developable boundary from a small-scale screenshot.

## Separate field delineation from permission

EPA describes wetland identification using evidence involving soils, vegetation and hydrology and explains the relationship to the Section 404 program. The project’s regulatory path requires a current, site-specific assessment. Federal jurisdiction is not the only potential layer of regulation, so ask the appropriate agencies about state and local requirements as well. [EPA wetland identification](https://www.epa.gov/cwa-404/how-wetlands-are-defined-and-identified-under-cwa-section-404).

Have a qualified consultant define the study area and deliverable: field delineation, report, mapped features and the agency coordination needed for the proposed activity. Ask whether seasonal conditions affect the work. Identify whether the resulting document is a consultant finding, an agency determination or a permit; these do not mean the same thing.

## Investigate alternatives before assuming a cure

A parcel may support your intended use through a different layout. Ask whether the building, road or service route can avoid sensitive areas and required buffers. Compare alternatives before treating fill or mitigation as a routine cost item. Their acceptability, design and timing require review and may remain uncertain.

**Hypothetical example:** a 15-acre parcel offers two potential house locations. The shorter driveway crosses a mapped wet area; the longer route avoids the preliminary area of concern but needs additional grading. The useful comparison includes field findings, approvals, construction scope and long-term maintenance for both routes. Gross acreage alone cannot select the better layout.

## Preserve a clear decision record

- State the exact activity proposed, including temporary work and access.
- Keep field findings tied to the parcel and the area actually studied.
- Record agencies consulted and distinguish advice from issued determinations.
- Show remaining approvals and contingencies in the project schedule.
- Carry the viable design costs into the acquisition budget.

Do not start clearing, draining or filling to make a site look more usable while these questions remain unresolved. Ask the responsible professionals and authorities what activity is allowed before authorizing work.
